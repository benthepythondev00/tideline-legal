How the calculator counts

It uses the same arithmetic a doctor would use on paper. Your next period is expected on the first day of your last period plus your cycle length. Ovulation is estimated by counting back your luteal length from that date, which is why the default is 14: the second half of the cycle tends to be steadier than the first, so most of the difference between a 26-day and a 34-day cycle happens before ovulation, not after it. The luteal phase is every day from the one after ovulation up to the day before your period.

The premenstrual week is the seven days just before your period. That is the window in the definition of PMDD: symptoms show up in that final week in most cycles, start to ease within a few days of the period starting, and are minimal or gone in the week after it. IAPMD describes the same pattern, with symptoms that can begin anywhere in the luteal phase and lift once the period arrives.

Why it is an estimate

IAPMD describes a luteal phase of about 12 to 14 days. The US Office on Women’s Health notes that the time from ovulation to the next period can run from about one week to 19 days, and cycles shift with stress, illness, travel, breastfeeding and the years before menopause. If your cycles vary by more than a few days, enter your average and read the dates as a range. If you have used ovulation tests or tracked temperature and know your own luteal length, put it in the third box.

Hormonal birth control changes the picture. Most combined pills, patches and rings stop ovulation, so there is no luteal phase to calculate, and the bleed in the break week is a withdrawal bleed. Some people still notice mood changes on a schedule; birth control and mood covers that.

What to do with your dates

  • Write a line every day, not only in the bad week. A PMDD diagnosis compares the week before your period with the week after it, so the good days are evidence too. Why doctors ask for two cycles explains why one month is not enough.
  • Put the premenstrual weeks in your calendar. Fewer deadlines, fewer hard conversations, more sleep. Plan your hard week has a simple way to do it.
  • Check your dates against what happened. When your period comes early or late, the next prediction moves with it. After a few cycles your real average is better than any default.
  • Bring the record, not the calculator. A doctor will want daily ratings over two cycles. The free printable PMDD chart works on paper.

If your luteal weeks bring thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the US, or your local emergency number. You don’t have to wait for the period to arrive for it to count.

Common questions

How do you calculate the luteal phase?

Add your cycle length to the first day of your last period to estimate your next period. Count back your luteal length (14 days if you don’t know it) to estimate ovulation. The luteal phase runs from the day after ovulation to the day before your period.

When is the PMDD window?

PMDD is judged on the final week before your period: symptoms are present in most cycles in that week, start to ease within a few days after the period starts, and are minimal or gone in the week after it.

How long is the luteal phase?

IAPMD describes about 12 to 14 days. The US Office on Women’s Health notes the time from ovulation to the next period can run from about one week to 19 days, so a calculator gives an estimate, not a date.

Can a calculator tell me if I have PMDD?

No. It shows when to pay attention. A PMDD diagnosis rests on daily symptom ratings over at least two cycles, compared between the week before your period and the week after it. The PMDD self-check asks the questions a doctor would start with.
