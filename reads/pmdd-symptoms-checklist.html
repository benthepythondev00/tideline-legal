PMDD symptoms checklist
and how diagnosis works

6 min read · checked against its sources · updated 13 Oct 2026

Premenstrual dysphoric disorder, PMDD, is described by IAPMD as a cyclical, hormone-based mood disorder. Hormone levels in people with PMDD are typical; what differs is how the brain reacts to their normal rise and fall after ovulation. The US Office on Women’s Health says it affects up to 5% of women of childbearing age.

PMDD is listed in the DSM-5, the manual doctors use to diagnose mental health conditions. This page puts its criteria into plain words, as IAPMD, the Office on Women’s Health and researchers summarize them. Use it to prepare for a conversation with a doctor. It isn’t a way to diagnose yourself.

The symptoms, area by area

There are eleven symptom areas. Four of them are about mood, and they carry the most weight:

  • Mood swings. Feeling suddenly sad or tearful, or much more sensitive to rejection.
  • Irritability or anger. Persistent, and leading to more conflict with the people around you.
  • Low mood. Feeling depressed or hopeless, or harsh on yourself, with guilt or a sense of worthlessness.
  • Anxiety. Feeling tense, anxious or on edge.

The other seven:

  • Less interest in usual activities, such as work, school, friends or hobbies
  • Trouble concentrating, or brain fog
  • Tiredness, low energy
  • Changes in appetite, overeating or food cravings
  • Sleeping much more or much less
  • Feeling overwhelmed or out of control
  • Physical symptoms, such as breast tenderness or swelling, bloating or weight gain, and joint or muscle pain

This list follows IAPMD’s symptom list and the Office on Women’s Health. IAPMD also marks feeling out of control as a core emotional symptom.

How many symptoms count

A diagnosis needs at least five of the eleven, and at least one of them has to come from the four mood areas. The Office on Women’s Health puts it as five or more symptoms including one about mood, and the C-PASS paper, which turns the DSM-5 criteria into a scoring method, says the same. So someone with strong cramps, bloating and tiredness but no change in mood would not meet the criteria, however rough those days are.

The timing rule

Timing is what makes PMDD what it is. As the C-PASS authors quote the criteria, symptoms are present in the week before the period, start to improve within a few days after it begins, and are minimal or gone in the week after. This has to hold in the majority of cycles. IAPMD describes the same shape: symptoms in the one to two weeks before a period that consistently resolve shortly after bleeding starts.

A symptom-free stretch after the period is part of the picture. If there is never a week when you feel like yourself, a doctor will look for other explanations.

It has to affect your life

The symptoms must cause real distress, or get in the way of work, school, usual social activities or relationships. That is the wording the C-PASS paper quotes from the criteria. Feeling a bit flat before a period is common; PMDD is when the week changes how you function.

It has to show up in daily ratings

This is the part people often don’t expect. The criteria say symptoms should be confirmed by prospective daily ratings during at least two symptomatic cycles. IAPMD calls this a requirement of the DSM-5 and recommends the DRSP, a daily chart. There are no lab tests for PMDD: blood, saliva, urine and hormone tests can’t show it.

What a doctor rules out

  • Look-alike conditions. IAPMD names major depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety disorders and borderline personality disorder as conditions that can be confused with PMDD.
  • A condition that flares before the period. When an ongoing condition such as depression gets worse premenstrually, IAPMD calls it premenstrual exacerbation, or PME. See PMDD or PME?
  • Other medical causes. MedlinePlus describes a full history, a physical and pelvic exam, thyroid tests and a psychiatric evaluation to rule other things out.
  • Hormonal contraception. IAPMD says PMDD should not be diagnosed in someone currently using hormonal contraceptives, since they can prevent ovulation.

Signs it may be something else

Going by the criteria above, PMDD is less likely if your symptoms are the same all month, if they don’t ease after your period starts, or if they are only physical. Period pain on its own isn’t part of it either: IAPMD notes that PMDD does not cause heavy periods, cramps or period pain, although cramping and bloating before a period are common. None of this means your symptoms don’t matter. It means a doctor should look in a different direction.

When it gets dark

Low mood in PMDD can be severe. IAPMD says PMDD can lead to suicidal thoughts and behaviors. If you are in danger right now, call or text 988 in the US, or your local emergency number. A plan for the darkest days shows how to prepare on a good day.

Common questions

What are the DSM-5 criteria for PMDD?

At least five of eleven symptoms, at least one of them about mood (mood swings, irritability or anger, depressed mood, anxiety), present in the week before the period, improving within a few days after it starts and minimal in the week after, in most cycles. They must cause distress or disrupt daily life, and be confirmed by daily ratings over at least two cycles.

How many symptoms do you need for PMDD?

Five or more, including at least one mood symptom, according to the US Office on Women’s Health and IAPMD.

Can a blood test diagnose PMDD?

No. IAPMD says there are no lab tests for PMDD. Tests may be used to rule out other conditions, such as thyroid problems, but the diagnosis rests on daily symptom ratings across at least two cycles.

Is PMDD in the DSM-5?

Yes. PMDD is a DSM-5 diagnosis, and IAPMD notes that the DSM-5 requires daily tracking across at least two cycles to confirm it.
